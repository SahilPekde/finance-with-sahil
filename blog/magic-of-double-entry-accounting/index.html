The Magic of Double-Entry Accounting

By Sahil Pekde · A practical introduction to debit, credit, capital, cash and accrual accounting.

Have accounting terms such as debit, credit, capital and assets ever confused you? The easiest way to understand them is to follow a real transaction through the books.

Every transaction has two sides

Imagine Mr. A invests ₹2,00,000 into his business bank account. The business now owns cash in the bank, and the owner's capital increases by the same amount.

AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Bank A/c2,00,000—
Capital A/c—2,00,000

Next, Mr. A buys a laptop for ₹50,000 from the business bank account. The laptop becomes a business asset and bank balance falls.

AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Computer Equipment A/c50,000—
Bank A/c—50,000

Remember: Debit does not always mean an increase, and credit does not always mean a decrease. Their effects depend on the account type. In a balanced journal entry, total debits equal total credits.

Meet Mr. A and Mr. B

Mr. A uses mercantile (accrual) accounting; his friend Mr. B uses cash accounting. Both deliver goods worth ₹80,000 on 30 March and receive payment in April.

BasisMarchApril
Mercantile / accrualRecognise ₹80,000 sales and a receivable, assuming revenue-recognition conditions are metRecord cash received and clear the receivable
CashNo sales revenue recognised yetRecognise ₹80,000 revenue when cash is received

Single-entry vs double-entry

Single-entry refers to simplified or incomplete bookkeeping records. Double-entry records the debit and credit effects of every transaction. This is different from the cash-versus-accrual choice, which concerns when income and expenses are recognised.

Which accounting basis should a business use?

Cash-basis records can be simpler for some small businesses, but the appropriate method depends on applicable laws, tax requirements and reporting needs. Companies in India generally prepare their financial statements on an accrual basis.
