FP&A and Modelling · Lesson 06

Integrated Three-Statement Financial Modelling

By Sahil Pekde · Connect projected profit, balance sheet and cash flow through consistent schedules.

Learning objective: Connect projected profit, balance sheet and cash flow through consistent schedules. Apply the method to a realistic business situation and explain the management decision it supports.

1. Why integration matters

A three-statement model links projected profit and loss, balance sheet and cash-flow statement. Revenue and operating assumptions flow into profit; depreciation and financing schedules affect profit and balances; working-capital movements convert profit to cash.

The model must satisfy the balance-sheet equation in every forecast period. A balancing “plug” that conceals errors is not a substitute for sound linkages.

2. Core supporting schedules

Prepare revenue, operating cost, fixed asset/depreciation, receivables, payables, inventory, borrowing, interest and tax schedules. Link receivables to credit sales and DSO; payables to purchases and DPO, with clearly stated conventions.

If annual credit revenue is ₹365 lakh and DSO is 30 days, an illustrative year-end receivable estimate using a 365-day assumption is ₹30 lakh. Actual balance depends on seasonality and collection patterns.

3. A small integrated example

Assume opening cash ₹20 lakh, no debt, no other working-capital balances and opening equity ₹20 lakh. The company earns ₹10 lakh cash revenue, pays ₹6 lakh cash operating expenses and buys equipment for ₹3 lakh cash. Ignore tax and depreciation for simplicity.

P&L profit = ₹4 lakh. Closing cash = 20 + 10 − 6 − 3 = ₹21 lakh. Closing equipment = ₹3 lakh. Total assets = ₹24 lakh. Closing equity = opening ₹20 lakh + profit ₹4 lakh = ₹24 lakh. The balance sheet balances.

4. Integrity checks

Use checks for assets − liabilities − equity = zero, opening cash + net cash movement = closing cash, debt roll-forward, depreciation and fixed asset movement, and retained earnings reconciliation. Document sign conventions and assumptions.

CFO perspective: A credible model shows how growth is funded and when financing or liquidity constraints appear—not just an attractive profit line.

Formula and worked application

Assets − Liabilities − Equity = 0; Closing cash = Opening cash + Net cash movement
Try it yourself: Opening cash ₹20 lakh, cash revenue ₹10 lakh, cash opex ₹6 lakh, equipment purchase ₹3 lakh: closing cash ₹21 lakh and equity ₹24 lakh.

Worked business case: Connect P&L, balance sheet and cash flow

A product company projects ₹100 lakh revenue, 60% COGS, ₹20 lakh operating expenses, ₹5 lakh depreciation, and ₹3 lakh interest.

Item / transactionAmount or entryMeaning
Revenue₹100 lakhP&L
COGS₹60 lakhP&L
Operating expenses excl. depreciation₹20 lakhP&L
EBITDA₹20 lakh100 − 60 − 20
Depreciation₹5 lakhNon-cash P&L expense
EBIT₹15 lakh20 − 5
Interest₹3 lakhFinance cost
Profit before tax₹12 lakh15 − 3
Financial interpretation: A three-statement model links depreciation to the fixed-asset schedule, interest to debt, receivables to revenue and collection assumptions, inventory to COGS, and cash to the cash flow statement. Tax and working-capital movements must be modelled separately.

Apply it yourself

Practice question: If EBITDA is ₹25 lakh and depreciation ₹4 lakh, what is EBIT?
Show worked answer

₹25 lakh − ₹4 lakh = ₹21 lakh.

Think like a CFO

Build explicit balance checks, cash roll-forwards, debt roll-forwards and scenario sensitivities; never plug cash merely to balance the model.
