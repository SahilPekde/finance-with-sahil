Preparing the P&L, Balance Sheet and Cash Flow Statement

By Sahil Pekde · Connect an adjusted trial balance to the three statements.

1. The three statements answer different questions

P&L: Did the business earn a profit over a period? Balance sheet: What does it own and owe on a date? Cash flow: Where did cash come from and where did it go during the period?

2. Build a miniature integrated example

Assume an owner contributes ₹2,00,000, buys equipment for ₹50,000 cash, earns ₹80,000 of credit sales, incurs ₹20,000 cash operating expenses, and records ₹5,000 depreciation. Ignore taxes and other costs for teaching purposes.

Profit and loss₹
Revenue80,000
Cash operating expenses(20,000)
Depreciation(5,000)
Profit55,000
Balance sheet at period-end₹
Bank1,30,000
Trade receivables80,000
Equipment net of depreciation45,000
Total assets2,55,000
Liabilities0
Owner capital2,00,000
Retained profit55,000
Total equity and liabilities2,55,000

3. Explain the cash flow

Operating cash outflow is ₹20,000 because the ₹80,000 sale remains unpaid. Investing cash outflow is ₹50,000 for equipment. Financing inflow is ₹2,00,000 capital contribution.

Cash-flow section₹
Operating activities(20,000)
Investing activities(50,000)
Financing activities2,00,000
Net increase in cash1,30,000

Cash closing balance ₹1,30,000 agrees with the balance sheet. Under an indirect operating cash-flow presentation: profit ₹55,000 + depreciation ₹5,000 − receivables increase ₹80,000 = negative ₹20,000.

4. Essential links between the statements

  • Net profit increases retained earnings (subject to distributions and other equity movements).
  • Depreciation reduces profit and the net carrying amount of equipment but is not a current-period cash outflow.
  • Changes in receivables, payables and inventory help reconcile profit to operating cash.
  • Cash flow closing balance must reconcile to cash and cash equivalents, allowing for classification and restricted cash considerations.
CFO perspective: Profitability, solvency and liquidity are different dimensions. A profitable company can still fail to pay its bills when cash conversion is poor.
