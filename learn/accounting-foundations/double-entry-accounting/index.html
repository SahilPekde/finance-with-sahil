Accounting Principles and Double-Entry Bookkeeping

By Sahil Pekde · Learn the debit-credit logic and how transactions change the accounts.

Have you ever heard words like debit, credit, capital, assets and liabilities and wondered what they really mean? You do not need to memorise complicated rules to begin. Follow a few everyday business transactions, and the logic becomes much clearer.

Meet Mr. A, who is starting a business, and Mr. B, who helps him understand how transactions are recorded. We will also compare how the same sale is recognised under accrual and cash accounting.

1. Mr. A starts his business with ₹2,00,000

Mr. A transfers ₹2,00,000 from his personal savings into his new business bank account. He thinks, “The business has received money, so is this income?”

Mr. B explains: No. The money is capital contributed by the owner, not revenue earned from customers. The business has gained a bank asset, and the owner's equity has increased by the same amount.

Journal entry — capital introduced
AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Bank A/c2,00,000—
Capital A/c—2,00,000
Total2,00,0002,00,000

What happened? Bank increased by ₹2,00,000 (debit), while owner's capital increased by ₹2,00,000 (credit). Both sides of the entry are equal.

2. Mr. A buys a laptop for ₹50,000

Next, Mr. A purchases a laptop for ₹50,000 using the business bank account. He asks whether the entire purchase should be treated as an expense.

Mr. B explains that a laptop used in the business over multiple accounting periods is generally recorded as a fixed asset, subject to the applicable accounting policy and materiality. Its cost is normally allocated over its useful life through depreciation.

Journal entry — laptop purchased
AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Computer Equipment A/c50,000—
Bank A/c—50,000
Total50,00050,000

The business now has a laptop worth ₹50,000 at cost and ₹1,50,000 remaining in the bank, ignoring other transactions and subsequent depreciation.

Key principle: Debit does not always mean “money received,” and credit does not always mean “money paid.” The effect depends on the type of account. Assets and expenses normally increase with debits; liabilities, equity and income normally increase with credits.

3. A sale happens on 30 March, but payment arrives in April

Now imagine Mr. A makes a qualifying credit sale of ₹80,000 on 30 March, but the customer pays in April. For simplicity, assume the sale meets the applicable revenue-recognition criteria and ignore GST and other taxes.

Mr. A uses accrual (mercantile) accounting

Under accrual accounting, the sale is recognised in March because that is when the revenue is earned under the assumed facts—not when the customer pays.

30 March — record the credit sale
AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Trade Receivables A/c80,000—
Sales A/c—80,000

April — receive the payment

AccountDebit (₹)Credit (₹)
Bank A/c80,000—
Trade Receivables A/c—80,000

Notice that April's collection does not create revenue again. It converts a receivable into cash.

Mr. B illustrates the cash basis

Mr. B now shows what would happen if a business used cash-basis accounting for the same transaction. No sales revenue would be recorded in March. The ₹80,000 would be recognised as revenue in April when the payment is received.

Accounting basisMarchApril
Accrual / mercantileRecognise ₹80,000 revenue and receivableCollect cash and settle receivable; no new revenue
Cash basisNo revenue recordedRecognise ₹80,000 revenue on receipt

Important distinction: Cash versus accrual describes when transactions are recognised. Single-entry versus double-entry describes how transactions are recorded. These are different concepts.

4. Why is it called double-entry accounting?

Every transaction affects at least two accounts. The total debit amount must equal the total credit amount. This helps keep the accounting equation in balance:

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity

After the three example transactions are recorded on an accrual basis, the business has the following simplified balances before depreciation, expenses, taxes or any other transactions:

AccountDebit balance (₹)Credit balance (₹)
Bank1,50,000—
Computer Equipment50,000—
Trade Receivables80,000—
Capital—2,00,000
Sales—80,000
Total2,80,0002,80,000

This is a simplified trial balance as at 30 March, before the customer pays in April. Total debits and credits agree at ₹2,80,000. However, a balanced trial balance alone does not prove that every transaction has been recorded correctly.

5. From journal entries to financial statements

The process does not stop at journal entries. Transactions are posted to ledger accounts, account balances are summarised in a trial balance, adjustments are made, and financial statements are prepared.

The accounting workflow

Business transaction → Journal entry → General ledger → Trial balance → Adjustments → Financial statements

For our simplified example, the profit and loss statement reports ₹80,000 in sales (before any expenses), while the balance sheet reflects the bank balance, laptop, trade receivable and owner's equity. The cash flow statement separately explains the cash movements.

6. What should a future CFO learn from this?

Recording an entry is only the beginning. A finance leader must also ask whether the transaction is classified correctly, whether the revenue belongs to the right period, whether receivables are collectible, and whether the financial statements can be reconciled to supporting evidence.

For example, a company may report strong sales in March but still face a cash shortage if customers pay in April or later. That is why profit is not the same as cash flow—and why working-capital management matters.
